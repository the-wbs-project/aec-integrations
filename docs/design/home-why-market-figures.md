# Home "why AECi" market figures — sourcing & recommendations

> **Issue:** AECI-285 · **Spec:** `STAGE_1_SPEC.md` §4.1 (the "why AECi / the problem" band) ·
> **Component:** `apps/web/src/app/home/home-why.ts` · **Direction:** `docs/design/unified-home-direction.md` (band 3) ·

## Why this document exists

The home "why AECi / the problem" band (built in AECI-272) shows three market figures, translated
from the legacy coming-soon landing (`apps/landing/public/index.html`, the "PROBLEM" section):

1. **≈34%** — "of reviews on major platforms are estimated to be AI-generated"
2. **$87K** — "a year is what vendors pay to boost their ranking on leading review sites"
3. **900+** — "tools lumped into generic categories with no way to filter by AEC discipline"

The landing recorded **no sources** for any of them; they were authored for marketing. The band currently
hedges with "≈" / "estimated to be" plus one note, `@@home.why.estimatesNote`: *"Figures are industry estimates."*

`PRODUCT.md` is explicitly anti-hype / substance-first, and the band's whole thesis is that other platforms
are dishonest about their numbers. Presenting our own **unsourced** figures on the launch marketing home
undercuts that. This document is the research record that closes the gap: for each figure, a citable source
or a defensible revision.

This doc does **not** by itself change the rendered figures. The copy + citation-treatment change is a
content decision; see **Recommendation** and **Open decisions** at the end.

## Figure 1 — "≈34% of reviews on major platforms are estimated to be AI-generated"

**Verdict: unsupported as written.** No study, report, or dataset found puts AI-generated reviews at ~34%
on "major platforms." The figure appears to be invented or mis-remembered.

**What the evidence actually says:**

- **AI-generated reviews, measured directly (best match for the exact claim):** Originality.AI's 2025 study
  of Google reviews (15 North American cities, 20 business categories, Google Places API) found the
  AI-generated rate rose from **5.01% (2019) to 19% (end of 2024)** — a **279.2%** increase. Their summary:
  *"the rapid rise … from 5.01% in 2019 to 19% by the end of 2024 signals a profound transformation in the
  digital review landscape."* Human-authored reviews still dominate at 81% in 2024.
  [originality.ai/blog/ai-google-reviews-study](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-google-reviews-study) *(fetched 2026-06-27)*
- **A lower bound on a different platform:** Pangram Labs found ~3% of 30,000 front-page Amazon reviews were
  AI-generated (≈5% in some categories like beauty/wellness), and 74% of the AI ones were 5-star.
  [inc.com — Fake AI Reviews Are Spreading Fast](https://www.inc.com/chris-morris/fake-ai-reviews-spreading-fast-what-businesses-can-do-about-it/91225246)
- **Fake reviews broadly (a superset of "AI-generated"):** the widely-repeated industry estimate is that
  **~30%** of online reviews are fake (sources cite a 30–40% range). This is the closest figure in *magnitude*
  to 34%, but it measures "fake," not specifically "AI-generated," so adopting it changes the claim's meaning.
  [reviewdriver.com — What % of online reviews are fake (2025)](https://www.reviewdriver.com/article/what-percentage-of-online-reviews-are-fake-in-2025) ·
  [capitaloneshopping.com — Fake Review Statistics (2026)](https://capitaloneshopping.com/research/fake-review-statistics/)

**Important caveat to disclose if we cite 19%:** Originality.AI measured **Google / local-business** reviews.
There is **no public study** measuring AI-generated reviews specifically on the **B2B software review sites**
that are AECi's actual subject (G2, Capterra, Gartner Peer Insights, TrustRadius). So 19% is an honest,
sourced *proxy* for "AI is now a material share of online reviews," not a measurement of software-review sites.

**Recommendation:** Replace "≈34%" with a sourced claim. In order of preference:

- **(A, recommended)** Keep the exact "AI-generated" meaning, anchor to Originality.AI. Either use the
  figure **"≈19%"** (Google reviews, 2024) or — stronger and equally honest — lead with the *trend*:
  *"AI-generated reviews on Google rose about 279% from 2019 to 2024."* Pair with a one-line disclosure that
  it covers Google/local reviews.
- **(B)** Pivot the claim to what *is* directly documented about the software-review-site villains:
  **vendor-funded / incentivized reviews** (G2 permits gift-card incentives up to $100, vendor-funded credits
  up to ~$1,000; Capterra labels incentivized reviews). This trades the AI angle for a more on-target one.
  [stackmatix.com — G2 & Capterra review strategy](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/g2-capterra-review-strategy)
- **(C)** Use the broad **"~30% of online reviews are fake"** figure — closest to 34% numerically, but
  softer sourcing and a changed claim ("fake" ≠ "AI-generated").

## Figure 2 — "$87K a year … what vendors pay to boost their ranking on leading review sites"

**Verdict: unsupported as a typical figure.** No source states $87K. Triangulating real vendor pricing, the
**median** annual spend is far lower (~$27K); $87K only appears at the *top* of the enterprise/multi-product band.

**What the evidence actually says:**

- **G2 (best citable anchor):** Vendr's marketplace data (512 real purchases) reports a **median vendor spend
  of $27,000/yr**. By tier: Starter $2,700–$3,000; Professional $13,500–$17,700; Enterprise $21,300–$28,300,
  rising to **$50,000–$95,000** with multiple products and add-ons (add-ons add 20–50%+). So **"$87K" sits
  inside the high enterprise band but is not a typical or median figure.**
  [vendr.com/marketplace/g2](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/g2) *(fetched 2026-06-27)*
- **Capterra:** an advertising channel — visibility is driven by **pay-per-click bidding** ($2/click minimum,
  $500/month minimum budget) or pay-per-lead; exact costs are opaque and negotiated.
  [getoden.com — G2 vs Capterra vs TrustRadius vs Gartner Peer Insights](https://getoden.com/blog/g2-vs-capterra-vs-trustradius-vs-gartner-peer-insights)
- **TrustRadius:** ~**$30,000/product/yr** public anchor — but TrustRadius positions itself as **no pay-to-play**
  (no incentivized reviews; ~48% of submissions rejected), so it is the *wrong* villain to cite for "buying a ranking."
- **Gartner — do not overclaim:** Magic Quadrant placement is **not purchasable**. This was tested in court
  (NetScout v. Gartner, 2014–2016; ruled Gartner does not operate pay-to-play). The real dynamic is
  **pay-to-access**: improving positioning requires paid analyst-advisory time. A claim that vendors "buy"
  a Gartner ranking is both false and legally fraught — avoid it.
  [starsight.biz — how much to be in a Magic Quadrant](https://www.starsight.biz/2022/05/09/how-much-do-i-need-to-pay-to-be-in-a-gartner-magic-quadrant-and-4-other-analyst-relations-myths/) ·
  [analystrelations.org (IIAR)](https://analystrelations.org/2016/03/11/do-you-need-to-pay-gartner-to-be-in-the-magic-quadrant/)
- **The pay-for-visibility critique is real and citable** (paid plans / PPC buy placement and "category leader"
  status; honest negative reviews of paying vendors reportedly suppressed):
  [LinkedIn — "Calling out G2 & Capterra: the dark side of app directories & reviews"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/calling-out-g2-capterra-dark-side-app-directories-reviews-moore-ev6wc)

**Recommendation:** Replace "$87K" with a citable, honestly-framed number:

- **(A, recommended)** **"~$27,000 a year"** — the median annual G2 vendor spend (Vendr, n=512). Specific,
  sourced, and defensible. Frame as paying for *visibility / a premium profile*, not "buying a ranking."
- **(B)** A **range** — e.g. *"$2,700 to $95,000+ a year"* — which tells the paywall story (visibility scales
  with spend) without resting on a single number.
- **(C)** Drop the dollar figure entirely and state the **mechanism**: top placement is sold via paid plans and
  pay-per-click bidding. Most defensible of all, but loses the "number" the stat-card format wants.

Either way, keep the claim about **G2/Capterra-style paid visibility**, and steer clear of implying Gartner's
Magic Quadrant is for sale.

## Figure 3 — "900+ tools lumped into generic categories with no way to filter by AEC discipline"

**Verdict: supported, and conservative.** This is the strongest of the three and can be cited directly.

**Evidence:**

- Capterra's **Construction Management** category alone lists **~986** products ("Capterra covers 986
  construction management software products"; 794 surfaced with filters on the main listing page) — i.e.
  **nearly a thousand tools in a single generic category**, with no facet for AEC discipline (architecture vs.
  structural vs. MEP vs. civil) or project phase. That undifferentiated lumping is exactly AECi's differentiator.
  [capterra.com/construction-management-software](https://www.capterra.com/construction-management-software/) *(fetched 2026-06-27)*

**Recommendation:** **Keep "900+"** — it is a safe floor below the actual ~986, so it stays true even as the
count drifts. Optionally sharpen the wording to name the source category, e.g. *"900+ tools in a single
generic 'construction management' category, with no way to filter by AEC discipline."* This is the one figure
that can be a **verifiable, dated citation** rather than an estimate.

## Citation treatment (AC #2)

> **Implemented:** a per-figure **"Source"** link with an accessible hover/focus citation reveal (see
> "Decisions taken"). The rationale below is retained as the record of why this beat the alternatives.

All three figures can now be tied to **datable sources** (one verifiable directory count, one primary study,
one marketplace dataset). That makes the honest move a graduation from the blanket *"Figures are industry
estimates"* to **lightweight per-figure attribution** — the small reusable "sources" pattern the issue
anticipates:

- Replace `@@home.why.estimatesNote` with a short **"Sources"** line (or superscript markers → a sources list)
  naming the origin of each figure, e.g. *"Sources: Originality.AI (2025); Vendr; Capterra (2026)."*
- Keep **"≈" / "estimated"** only where the figure is genuinely a proxy (figure 1). Figures 2 and 3 can be
  stated plainly once revised, because they rest on real pricing data and a live category count.
- Treatment stays within the AECI-272 / `DESIGN.md` constraints: no hero-metric template, Clay ≤5%, borders
  not shadows, sentence case, no em dashes, `@@` i18n ids, axe-clean. A sources line is plain text — no new tokens.

If the team prefers **not** to add footnotes to the marketing home, the honest fallback is: swap figures 1 and 2
to the *defensible* numbers (≈19% / ~$27K), keep figure 3 as the verifiable count, and replace the
"industry estimates" note with the single sourced line above. Either path removes the "unsourced figures" problem.

## Decisions taken (implemented 2026-06-27)

Chris's calls, now shipped in `apps/web/src/app/home/home-why.ts`:

1. **Figure 1 → option A.** Revised ≈34% to **≈19%** (AI-generated Google reviews, Originality.AI); the
   description is scoped honestly to Google with the 2019 → 2024 rise.
2. **Figure 2 → option A.** Revised $87K to **$27K** (median annual G2 vendor spend, Vendr); framed as "paid
   visibility," not "buying a ranking."
3. **Treatment.** Each figure carries a small **"Source"** link to its citation. The citation is revealed on
   hover and on keyboard focus, and announced to screen readers via `aria-describedby` — a CSS-only reveal, so
   the band stays static and edge-cache-neutral. The blanket `@@home.why.estimatesNote` is retired.
4. **Figure 3** kept at 900+ (verified, conservative), now with its Capterra source link.

Verification: the component spec (`home-why.component.spec.ts`), `tsc`, ESLint, Prettier, and the impeccable P0
detector all pass; the preview playground (`unified-home-preview.ts`) figures were corrected in lockstep. A full
axe/visual a11y pass runs in CI before merge.
